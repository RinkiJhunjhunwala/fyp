\begin{center}
    {\Large \textbf{Abstract}\par}
    \vspace{0.4in}
\end{center}

Heart attacks, scientifically known as Acute Myocardial Infarction (AMI), are one of the most common and dangerous medical emergencies around the world. Every year, millions of patients are rushed to intensive care units (ICUs) with severe chest pain and cardiovascular distress. If doctors can catch the early warning signs quickly, patient survival improves drastically. Modern hospitals record vital signs like heart rate, blood pressure, and oxygen saturation continuously by the bedside. In theory, machine learning could analyze these numbers to assist doctors. However, there is a huge roadblock in the real world: \textbf{patient data privacy}. Hospital data is protected by strict laws like HIPAA in the United States, GDPR in Europe, and the DPDP Act in India. Different hospitals are legally forbidden from uploading their raw patient records to a central server or sharing them with each other. As a result, small or rural hospitals with few patients are left with very small datasets, making it impossible for them to build accurate machine learning models on their own.

In this undergraduate final year project, we design, build, test, and deploy a complete, working solution to this problem using \textbf{Federated Learning}. Using the publicly available MIMIC-IV version 3.1 critical care database, we identified patients admitted with cardiovascular conditions using official medical billing codes (ICD-9 code 410 and ICD-10 codes I21--I22). From an initial set of 90 candidate patients, our careful data audit revealed that 10 patients had readings recorded outside the ICU monitor charts. Rather than guessing or faking data, we filtered our dataset to 80 verified ICU patients, perfectly split into 40 AMI patients and 40 control patients.

To make this practical for standard machine learning, we took the first 24 hours of routine bedside vital signs---Heart Rate (HR), Systolic Blood Pressure (SBP), Diastolic Blood Pressure (DBP), and Oxygen Saturation ($\text{SpO}_2$)---and transformed them into 43 simple statistical and trend features per patient. This converted thousands of irregular, messy time-series readings into a clean, single-row summary for each patient without any data leakage.

We then carried out two major experiments:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Centralized Machine Learning Comparison:} We tested six popular algorithms: Logistic Regression, Random Forest, Decision Tree, Support Vector Machine (SVM), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), and Gradient Boosting using 5-fold cross-validation. We discovered an important lesson: simpler models worked much better than complex models. Simple regularized Logistic Regression won with \textbf{68.75\% test accuracy and 0.7188 ROC-AUC}, whereas fancy models like Random Forest (62.5\%) and KNN (50.0\%) struggled because they overfit to the small number of patients.
    \item \textbf{Federated Learning Across Hospitals:} We simulated three separate hospital branches: Hospital A (32 patients), Hospital B (a small rural clinic with only 16 patients), and Hospital C (16 patients). When Hospital B tried to train a model all by itself, it completely failed, collapsing to \textbf{31.25\% accuracy} and failing to catch a single heart attack. But when we connected the three hospitals using the Federated Averaging (FedAvg) algorithm, Hospital B was saved: the federated model reached \textbf{68.75\% accuracy (IID) and 75.00\% accuracy (Non-IID)}, matching the centralized performance while keeping 100\% of raw patient data strictly inside each hospital's local walls.
\end{enumerate}

Finally, we built a fully working web application using Python and Flask. Doctors or nurses can enter patient vitals, choose between centralized or federated models, and get an instant risk assessment with simple explanations. This report proves that hospitals can safely collaborate and build strong medical AI without compromising patient privacy.
